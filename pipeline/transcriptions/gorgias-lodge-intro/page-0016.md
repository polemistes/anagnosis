Gorgias was not a cheap teacher; his charge was 100 minæ for each pupil. It seems, therefore, strange that he should have left only 1000 staters, or 200 minæ, behind him when he died; that too, when he had neither wife nor family to spend his money for him. But although he lived simply, he loved parade and to make an imposing display in public. In imitation of the priestly garb of his old master, Empedocles, he used to wear a purple mantle and golden sandals.[^13] It is said also that he had a golden statue of himself set up at Delphi, at the dedication of which he delivered a great oration.[^14] Otherwise even his enemies could find nothing in his manner of life to criticise, and were obliged to feel the highest respect for him. To his simple manner of life is attributed the fact that he not only reached a very great old age, but retained his bodily freshness and vigor until the end. Anecdotes are related of him to show his preference for old age instead of a disgust at it.[^15] When finally his death was approaching, — a death which was without sickness and rather like a falling asleep, — he is said to have murmured, ‘Now Sleep is beginning to conduct me to his brother Death.’

**§ 6.** Gorgias left behind him various writings, six speeches — he seems not to have written a work on rhetoric — and a philosophical treatise περὶ φύσεως ἢ περὶ τοῦ μὴ ὄντος. It is not certain that any of these have come down to us.
