## 425
**᾿Αντίλοχος**. Pindar is our earliest authority for the story of Antilochus saving his father Nestor’s life : he brings it in ἃ propos of a son who had driven his father’s chariot in the Pythian games, and won the race (Pytk. 6. 38 ff.). Memnon was pressing Nestor hard, and one of the horses in Nestor’s chariot had been wounded by Paris. Nestor called for help to Antilochus, who diverted Memnon’s attack from his father to himself, and was killed; thus winning the fame, ὕπατος ἀμφὶ τοκεῦσιν ἔμμεν πρὸς ἀρετάν. The Odyssey notices that Antilochus was slain by Memnon, but does not say that he fell in saving his father (4. 188). At the end of the //iad Antilochus is still living (23. 785 ff.); in ZI. 8. go it is Diomedes who rescues Nestor (from Hector). Pindar’s source was the Aethiopss of Arctinus, in which Achilles avenged Antilochus by slaying Memnon.

**ὃς παρῆν γόνος**, the son who was at his side :—not (I think) with direct reference to the saving of Nestor’s life by Antilochus,—this is more than παρῆν could suggest, without further explanation (cp. 373),—but rather in the general sense that the son was the stay and comfort of his father’s old age.—The Ms. reading, ὅσπερ ἦν γόνος, would clearly imply that Antilochus was Nestor’s only (or last surviving) son. The Ziad describes Nestor as having two sons at Troy, Thrasymedes and Antilochus (17. 378); and according to the Odyssey (3. 413 ff.) six sons were left to Nestor after the death of Antilochus, one of these being Thrasymedes. If it be suggested that the Aetkiopis may have represented Antilochus as the last surviving son, we may reply that this is extremely improbable, when it is remembered that several Ionian colonies claimed to have been founded by the Neleidae, descendants of Nestor who emigrated from Pylus (/ntrod. to Homer, p- 167). The same consideration condemns Seyffert’s ὅς γ᾽ ἔτ᾽ ἦν. Cavallin’s ὅς ποτ᾽ ἦν is free from this objection, but is somewhat weak.—See Appendix.

## 426
**δύ᾽** ad τώδ᾽ ἄνδρ᾽ ἔλεξας, a correction which I published in 1869 (see cr. n.), still appears to me the most probable. Porson’s δύ᾽ αὖ τώδ᾽ ἐξέδειξας is founded on the schol. in L, yp. δύ᾽ αὐτὼ δ᾽ ἐξέδειξας, and may be deemed certain so far as the words δύ᾽ αὖ τώδ᾽ are concerned. But no one has justified the use of ἐξέδειfas. We see the proper uses of the word in *O. C.* τοῦτ ἵν᾽ αὐτὸς ἐκδείξῃς ἐμοί (point them out, discover them, to me): ZZ. 348 τὸ τούτων μῖσος ἐκδείξειας dv (‘manifest’). Eur. Hipp. 1298 παιδὸς ἐκδεῖξαι φρένα | τοῦ σοῦ δικαίαν. But here the word is strangely inappropriate, ‘thou hast pointed out,’ instead of, ‘thou hast named.” And ἔλεξας, the most natural word, is in all the mss. It seems very rash, then, to assume, on the strength of the schol., that ἔλεξας is spurious, and ἐξέδειξας genuine, especially when we remember the quality of some of the variants which rest on the same authority; e.g., in v. 423, the schol. on κάκ᾽ ἐξήρυκε gives yp. κἀξεκήρυξεν.
