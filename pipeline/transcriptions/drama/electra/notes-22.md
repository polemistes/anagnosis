## 1357
**ὦ**. χεῖρες: she takes his hands in her own. This explains why the poet has not written φιλτάτας.. χεῖρας : we see, too, how natural is the transition to ἔχων, as she is looking in her old friend’s face. The sense is, ἔχων πόδας of τὰ ἥδιστα ὑπηρέτησαν, viz., in the journeys to and from Phocis.

## 1359
**οὐδ᾽ ἔφαινες**, ‘and didst not give any light.” This absolute use of the word is sufficiently interpreted by the context; it is scarcely needful to supply (e.g.) τὴν ἀλήθειαν, or συνόντα σεαυτόν .

**φαίνω** is never really intrans. (= ‘to appear’) except in the epic aor. φάνεσκον (/7. 11. 64, Od. 12. 241, etc.).—For ἀλλά με cp. Ai. 361.

## 1361
**πατέρα**. This is the only tragic trimeter in which the third foot is formed by a single word of three short syllables. When the third foot is a tribrach there is usu. a caesura both in the third and in the fourth foot (as O. 7: 248 κακὸν κακῶς vw ἄμορον ἐκτρῖψαι βίον: cp. Eur. 770. 497): or at least in the third foot (as “4,2. 31). But it should be observed that the pause after χαῖρ᾽, ὦ πάτερ makes a vital difference. The movement of the verse begins afresh at πατέρα, and the effect of that word to the ear is like that of a tribrach in the first, rather than in the third, place of a trimeter. Hence we may defend the text here, and yet concede that no tragic poet could have written such a verse without the pause (e.g. ἦλθ᾽ ἄσμενος πατέρα ποτ’ εἰσορᾶν δοκῶν).
