## 1320
**οὐκ ἂν δυοῖν ἥμαρτον**, *i.e.*, would have secured one of the two things. Classical Greek idiom preferred this negative form to a positive (such as δυοῖν θατέρου ἂν ἔτυχον). The modes of stating the dilemma vary; thus we find: (1) ἢ γὰρ..ἢ, as here; Andoc. or. 1 § 4 δυοῖν..οὐκ ἦν αὐτῷ ἁμαρτεῖν· ἢ γὰρ..μηνῦσαι..ἢ ἀποκτεῖναι: so Dem. or. 19 § 151, etc. (2) ἢ..ἢ, as Thuc. 1. 33 § 3 μηδὲ δυοῖν φθάσαι ἁμάρτωσιν (not fail to be beforehand with us in one of two things), ἢ κακῶσαι..ἢ βεβαιώσασθαι n. ## 1361
**πατέρα**. This is the only tragic trimeter in which the third foot is formed by a single word of three short syllables. When the third foot is a tribrach there is usu. a caesura both in the third and in the fourth foot (as *O. T.* 248 κακὸν κακῶς νιν ἄμορον ἐκτρῖψαι βίον: cp. Eur. *Tro.* 497): or at least in the third foot (as *Ant.* 31). But it should be observed that the pause after **χαῖρ’, ὦ πάτερ** makes a vital difference. The movement of the verse begins afresh at **πατέρα**, and the effect of that word to the ear is like that of a tribrach in the first, rather than in the third, place of a trimeter. Hence we may defend the text here, and yet concede that no tragic poet could have written such a verse without the pause (*e.g.* ἦλθ’ ἄσμενος πατέρα ποτ’ εἰσορᾶν δοκῶν).
